# Professional Identity & Relevance — Theory

## 1. Identity as a Priced Claim

Every sentence a hiring reader sees about you is a claim with two prices on
it: an evidence price (what would convince a skeptic) and a relevance price
(how much the reader's current need discounts it). "Experienced backend
engineer" prices low on both: no artifact, no specificity. "Cut p99 latency
on the order ingestion path from 850ms to 190ms by batching Kafka publishes"
prices high on evidence (numbers, named path, patent mechanism) and only
needs one matching job to be fully relevant.

## 2. The Middle Term: Relevance Is Not Flattery

A claim without relevance reads like capability tourism. Relevance is the
mapping you construct between what you did and the constraint set of the job
you want. The mapping has to be real: the order-ingestion claim above is
relevant when the target team owns latency-sensitive pipelines, and decorative
when they own billing workflows. Positioning is choosing your audience before
choosing your adjectives.

## 3. Levels: What Changes With Seniority

The claim stays a claim; the unit of evidence scales. A [Junior] engineer
prices identity on execution: things shipped, bugs isolated, tools learned
fast, tests written without being told. A [Senior] engineer prices identity
on judgment and ownership: trade-offs you made and owned, systems you kept
alive at 3 a.m., projects you talked a team out of — plus all junior-scale
evidence in your history. It is not two different selves; it is which scale
of outcome you can defend.

## 4. Titles vs. Function

Job titles are local tokens with company-specific meanings; "Lead", "Senior",
"Member of Technical Staff" do not cross borders cleanly. Your function does:
what you built, operated, tested, or prevented. State function first, title
second, and only when the title adds information.

## 5. The Trust Ledger

Every claim you make is a row in a ledger the reader audits as they scan.
Claims they can verify go in black ink; overclaims go in red and bleed onto
neighboring rows. An engineer who says "expert" in four frameworks and
shipped one is discounted on all rows including the true ones. The corrective
reflex is honest scoping: fluent, comfortable, familiar, exposed-to — graded
words cost nothing and save the ledger.

## 6. Identity Decay

Claims rot. A 2019 claim of "modern React practices" is a liability if you
have not shipped React since: it tells a careful reader your self-description
has not been maintained in years. The fix is a quarterly identity audit:
reread every public self-description, delete stale claims, reprice surviving
ones against what you shipped in the interval. Maintenance is the difference
between a profile and a fossil.

## 7. The One-Liner Disclosure

Every identity eventually compresses to one spoken sentence. Its structure
that survives actual hiring rooms: [level/function] + [domain] + [artifact
proof]. "I'm a QA automation technician; I test payment terminals; I built
the regression harness that our three firmware teams share." Say it flat,
no adjectives that require trust you have not yet earned.

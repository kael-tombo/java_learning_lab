# Why Causal Inference Matters

Money and lives ride on causal claims. Approving an ineffective drug
wastes billions and harms patients; blocking an effective one costs
lives. Product teams that mistake eager-user bias for feature impact
invest quarters in dead ends. Wage studies move legislation covering
millions - the design quality is policy quality.

Simpson's paradox is not academic: hospital rankings, school ratings,
and hiring funnels all pool over differing mixes, and pooled
leaderboards routinely punish whoever serves the hardest cases. Any
analyst who cannot stratify will, sooner or later, recommend harming
the exact group they meant to help.

The assumption discipline scales to teams. "Adjusted diff -0.01,
assuming no unmeasured confounding" fits in one line yet tells a
reviewer exactly what to attack: the assumption, not the arithmetic.
That focuses debate where it belongs and stops precision theater -
ever-tighter intervals around the wrong number.

Finally, causal literacy protects science itself: pre-registered trials,
parallel-trend plots, and sensitivity notes are what separate evidence
from advocacy with a dataset.

## Who pays for bad causality

Patients (wrong drugs), users (harmful features shipped on eager-user
bias), workers (wage policy from confounded studies). Causal errors do
not stay academic; they dose, deploy, and legislate.

## What good practice buys

Pre-registered trials stop outcome shopping; balance tables expose
failed matches before publication; sensitivity notes convert fragile
findings into honest maybes. Each practice trades a little narrative
cleanliness for a lot of truth - always worth it.

## The compounding margin

A team that states assumptions attracts the right criticism (attack the
license, not the arithmetic) and learns faster per study. Over years,
assumption-literate organizations accumulate real causal knowledge while
others accumulate significant p-values.

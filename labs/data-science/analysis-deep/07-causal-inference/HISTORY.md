# History - Causal Inference

Only widely documented facts; uncertain details are omitted.

## Randomized experiments

- R.A. Fisher's agricultural trials at Rothamsted in the 1920s and his
  "The Design of Experiments" (1935) established randomization,
  replication, and blocking. Random assignment as the basis for causal
  claims starts here.
- The 1948 British streptomycin trial for tuberculosis is widely cited
  as a landmark randomized medical trial, spreading RCTs into medicine.

## Observational methods

- Cochran and Cox's "Experimental Designs" (1950s) systematized design
  principles for non-laboratory settings.
- Rosenbaum and Rubin's propensity score (1983) gave matching a
  one-number summary of covariates; the Rubin causal model (potential
  outcomes) supplied the formal language this lab uses.
- Card and Krueger's minimum-wage study (1994) popularized
  difference-in-differences in economics; Angrist and co-authors spread
  instrumental-variables practice, recognized in later Nobel citations.

## Textbooks that consolidated the field

- Angrist and Pischke's "Mostly Harmless Econometrics" (2009) and
  "Mastering 'Metrics" (2014) made quasi-experimental designs standard
  curriculum. Pearl's "Causality" (2000/2009) formalized graphical
  models. See REFERENCES.md.

## Lesson

Each advance narrowed what may be claimed: Fisher demanded
randomization, Rubin demanded stated assumptions, the modern texts
demand the assumption printed next to the number.

## Reading the lineage

Fisher (randomize), Rubin (state assumptions), Angrist-Pischke (designs
for when you cannot randomize), Pearl (formalize the graphs). Each layer
constrains the previous one's over-claims - progress here means narrower
sentences, not bigger ones.

## What replication crises added

Failed replications across medicine and social science hardened the
discipline: pre-registration, balance tables, sensitivity notes. The
modern causal report is armored precisely where past claims broke.

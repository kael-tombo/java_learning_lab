# Lab 10: Mock Interview — A/B Testing & Experimentation

**Role**: ML Platform Engineer / Experimentation Engineer
**Duration**: 60 minutes
**Focus**: Two-proportion z-tests, p-values and confidence intervals, sample-size/power analysis, multi-armed bandits (epsilon-greedy, UCB, Thompson sampling)

---

**Interviewer**: "Start with the statistical core. How does `zTestTwoProportions` work, and what assumptions does it make?"

**Candidate**: "It's the classic two-proportion z-test. Each arm reports successes and impressions; we estimate `pA = successesA / nA` and `pB = successesB / nB`, then pool under the null hypothesis — `pPooled = (successesA + successesB) / (nA + nB)` — because if the null were true, both groups share one rate, so the pooled estimate is the most efficient variance estimate. The standard error is `sqrt(pPooled * (1 - pPooled) * (1/nA + 1/nB))`, and the z-statistic is the difference in rates divided by that SE — the signal-to-noise ratio of the experiment. The assumptions: independent users, no user in both groups, a normal approximation that holds when both groups are large enough (rule of thumb: at least 5 successes and 5 failures per cell — we have 600+, fine). The lab hashes `userId` to assign variants, which is what guarantees independence and no double-assignment."

**Interviewer**: "Walk through the p-value computation — why not just call a library?"

**Candidate**: "`pValue(z)` implements the Abramowitz-Stegun approximation of the standard normal CDF: with `a = |z|`, `p = 1 / (1 + a * 0.2316419)`, it evaluates a rational-polynomial expression and returns the two-tailed tail probability `2 * (1 - cdf)`. It's accurate to about 7 decimal places for reasonable z — more than enough for experiment decisions. No library because the lab is deliberately dependency-free — this is the exact formula I'd verify against before trusting a third-party stat package anyway. The `Math.min(cdf, 1.0)` clamp guards the numeric edge cases. And the important habit: the p-value is not the effect size — the demo prints `z=-0.940, p=0.3471 (not significant)`, and the right read is 'no evidence the challenger differs', not 'evidence they're equal'."

**Interviewer**: "The demo runs 5000 users per variant but finds no significance despite true rates of 12% vs 14%. Is the test broken?"

**Candidate**: "No — it's the variance of sampling. The observed rates came out 0.1210 vs 0.1272 — the difference shrank from the true 2 points to 0.62 points because the seeded random stream happened to under-represent the challenger's true 14% rate (636/5000). With z=-0.94, p=0.35, a correct analyst would *not* ship — but they'd also *not* conclude the challenger is worse. The deeper point: 5000 users per group with a 12% baseline gives a minimum detectable effect of about 1.8% at power 80% — the demo is underpowered for detecting the true 2-point gap. That's exactly why `minimumDetectableEffect` and the sample-size math must be run *before* launch, not after."

**Interviewer**: "Explain `minimumDetectableEffect` and the power story behind it."

**Candidate**: "MDE is the smallest true effect the experiment could detect with confidence: `(zAlpha + zBeta) * sqrt(2 * variance / n)`, where `zAlpha` is the critical value for the significance level (1.96 for 5%) and `zBeta` for power (0.84 for 80%). The `sqrt(2 * variance / n)` is the standard error of the *difference* of two independent proportions — the factor 2 is the two groups' variances summing. For n=5000 and variance `0.12 * 0.88`, MDE = 0.0182, i.e. 1.8%. The lab's simple version fixes z values per alpha/beta; production code would invert the formula to solve for n given a target MDE, and use `0.5 * (zAlpha + zBeta)² * variance / MDE²` per group."

**Interviewer**: "Now the bandit. Walk through epsilon-greedy, UCB1, and Thompson sampling as implemented — and their tradeoffs."

**Candidate**: "Epsilon-greedy: with probability epsilon explore a random arm, otherwise exploit `argmax(values)`. It's the baseline — simple, robust, and wasteful, because exploration is uniform even after an arm is clearly bad. UCB1: never explores uniformly; each arm's score is `values[i] + sqrt(2 * ln(totalPlays) / counts[i])` — the empirical mean plus a confidence bonus that shrinks as the arm is played. It tries every arm once (`counts[i] == 0` returns immediately — forced exploration), then automatically explores arms with high uncertainty and exploits arms with high means. Thompson sampling is the Bayesian version: each arm's reward distribution is Beta(α, β) with α = successes + 1, β = failures + 1; each round you *sample* one Beta per arm and play the highest sample — exploration emerges naturally because uncertain arms have wide, overlapping Beta densities. It handles changing environments and prior knowledge better than UCB. The tradeoffs: epsilon-greedy is easiest to reason about, UCB is deterministic and worst-case optimal, Thompson is usually best in practice with the fewest regrets — at the cost of needing a Gamma sampler (the lab's Marsaglia-Tsang)."

**Interviewer**: "The lab's bandit demo uses only epsilon-greedy in `main`, yet the class implements all three. What would you test if you were extending the demo to UCB or Thompson?"

**Candidate**: "First, correctness of the incremental mean update: `values[arm] = ((n-1) * values[arm] + reward) / n` must equal the running average — I'd assert that against a reference cumulative computation. For UCB I'd check forced exploration: the first 5 plays must hit all 5 arms. For Thompson I'd check the Beta sampler against known moments — mean α/(α+β) — and that with true rates `{0.10, 0.12, 0.15, 0.13, 0.11}` after 2000 rounds, arm 2 (the true best) receives the overwhelming majority of plays — in the walkthrough's seeded epsilon-greedy run it gets 1681 of 2000. Finally, I'd verify the exploration budget: with ε=0.1, about 200 plays should scatter across suboptimal arms (58, 48, 56, 157 here — plausible). These are the acceptance criteria I'd encode before promoting any bandit to production."

**Interviewer**: "Regret — what is it, and why does the demo's arm-2 allocation demonstrate it?"

**Candidate**: "Regret is the cumulative difference between the reward you actually collected and the reward an oracle would collect by always playing the true best arm — it's the cost of learning. In the demo, arm 2 has true rate 0.15 and gets 1681 plays; the 319 plays spent on the other four arms at ~0.08-0.13 rates are the regret. The goal of every bandit algorithm is to make regret grow sublinearly in time — logarithmic for UCB and Thompson, which is why they beat a fixed epsilon-greedy that keeps wasting epsilon on known-bad arms forever. For a recommendation or model-selection loop, this is the difference between 'explore forever' and 'settle on the winner'."

**Interviewer**: "When would you use a fixed A/B test instead of a bandit — and vice versa? The lab's Q4."

**Candidate**: "A/B test when you need a *statistical verdict* on a policy change: a model swap, a pricing change, a UI redesign — where the decision is binary, the stakes are high, and the organization needs the evidence trail (p-value, confidence interval, power analysis) to commit. Bandit when you need to *optimize an ongoing allocation* among many candidates: which of five models serves each request, which headline shows to which segment — where pulling the losing arms out early is itself the goal. The lab's framing is exactly right: A/B testing is for decision-making, bandits for optimization. And the guardrail: a bandit with high traffic and unequal allocation makes inference hard afterward — if you later want to *report* the winner's effect, you need either the A/B structure or post-hoc correction like inverse propensity weighting."

**Interviewer**: "Talk through `assignVariant`: hashing user IDs for deterministic bucketing. What could go wrong?"

**Candidate**: "`idx = Math.abs(Long.hashCode(userId)) % variants.size()` — deterministic, stable across requests, no cookies needed. Failure modes: `Long.hashCode` is a 32-bit hash of a 64-bit id, so collisions are possible (not harmful for bucketing — just a small imbalance); `Math.abs(Integer.MIN_VALUE)` stays negative — a famous edge case — and `% variants.size()` would then return a negative index, so the defensive version masks or takes `Integer.MIN_VALUE + 1` first; and the biggest one: changing the hash or the number of variants mid-experiment reassigns users — contaminating both groups. The production pattern is a stable `HashFunction` (Murmur) over a namespace-scoped salt — `userId + experimentName` — so the same user in two experiments gets independent assignments, and users never migrate between groups."

**Interviewer**: "The demo prints `95% CI=[0.1120, 0.1300]` for the champion. How is that interval built, and what does '95%' mean here?"

**Candidate**: "`confidenceInterval` uses the Wald interval: `p ± z * sqrt(p(1-p)/n)` with z=1.96 at alpha=0.05 (2.576 at 0.01). It's the standard normal quantile times the proportion's standard error. The interpretation trap: 95% means that if we re-ran the experiment many times, ~95% of the constructed intervals would contain the true rate — it is *not* a 95% probability that the true rate lies in this particular interval. Practically: the challenger's interval `[0.1180, 0.1364]` overlaps the champion's `[0.1120, 0.1300]` — overlapping intervals don't imply significance (or non-significance) directly, which is why the lab computes the z-test on the *difference* rather than eyeballing intervals."

**Interviewer**: "What's the sample size for detecting a 1-point lift at 5% significance, 80% power, 12% baseline? And the lab's Q1 sample-size formula?"

**Candidate**: "Using the standard formula `n = (zAlpha + zBeta)² * 2 * p(1-p) / (lift)²` per group: `(1.96 + 0.84)² * 2 * 0.12 * 0.88 / 0.01²` — that's 7.84 * 0.2112 / 0.0001 ≈ 16,600 per group, over 33,000 total. The lab's `minimumDetectableEffect` is the inverse view: for a given n, what effect can you detect. The practical point the interview notes make: run the power analysis before the experiment, budget for the traffic, and if the team can't reach the sample size, shrink the MDE target — or change the question."

**Interviewer**: "How do you validate the whole framework end-to-end — given the lab's assertion suggestions in INTERVIEW.md?"

**Candidate**: "Three assertion tiers. Statistical: seeded simulation with *known* conversion rates — run `zTestTwoProportions` on generated data, and the p-value must shrink as the true effect or sample size grows; I'd assert that with a 14% vs 12% true gap and 50,000 users the test says significant. Structural: the lab's own invariant checks — equal impressions across variants when assignment is random, per-variant counts that sum to the total, rates in [0,1]. Behavioral: the bandit must converge to the true-best arm — with `{0.10, 0.12, 0.15, 0.13, 0.11}`, arm index 2 should receive the most plays after 2000 rounds. These three tiers — statistical correctness, structural invariants, behavioral convergence — are exactly what I'd CI-gate, mirroring the validation gate from Lab 09."

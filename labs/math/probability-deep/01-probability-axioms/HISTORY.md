# History: Probability Axioms

## Games of Chance Before the Axioms

**Gerolamo Cardano (1501–1576)** wrote *Liber de Ludo Aleae* around 1564, counting dice outcomes by hand; it was published only in 1663. **Blaise Pascal (1623–1662)** and **Pierre de Fermat (1601–1665)** exchanged letters in the summer of 1654 on the problem of points, deciding that a fair stake depends on the expected value of the unfinished outcomes — the first use of expectation to settle a decision.

**Jacob Bernoulli (1655–1705)**'s *Ars Conjectandi* (published 1713 by his nephew Nikolaus) contains the weak law of large numbers: the ratio of successes converges to the true probability. Bernoulli also analyzed the probability of at least one head in coin combinations, using inclusion-exclusion on 17th-century urn problems.

## From Favorable Cases to Inverse Probability

**Pierre-Simon Laplace (1749–1827)**, in *Théorie analytique des probabilités* (1812), defined probability as favorable cases over all cases *without* requiring equally likely outcomes, and stated the rule of succession. **Siméon-Denis Poisson (1781–1840)** used the phrase "loi des grands nombres" in 1837.

**Thomas Bayes (1701–1761)**'s essay on inverse probability was read to the Royal Society by **Richard Price** in 1763 (published 1763), giving the formula that bears Bayes's name. **Pafnuty Chebyshev (1821–1894)** proved in 1867 the inequality that bounds tail probabilities using only the mean and variance — the tool that made "rare event" statements rigorous.

## Kolmogorov's Axioms (1933)

**Andrey Kolmogorov (1903–1987)** published *Grundbegriffe der Wahrscheinlichkeitsrechnung* in 1933, defining probability as a normalized countably additive measure on a σ-algebra. His three axioms — P(Ω) = 1, P(A) ≥ 0, and countable additivity over disjoint unions — let conditional probability, independence and expectation be *derived* instead of argued about.

## Competing Interpretations

**John von Mises (1883–1953)** defended the frequentist interpretation in *Wahrscheinlichkeit, Statistik und Wahrheit* (1928). **Bruno de Finetti (1906–1985)** argued in the 1930s that only exchangeable sequences support probability statements at all, and that probability is subjective. **R. T. Cox (1946)** showed that any consistent plausibility algebra must isomorphically satisfy the probability calculus; **Leonard J. Savage (1917–1971)** derived personal probability from rational preference axioms in *The Foundations of Statistics* (1954).

The axioms survive all three camps: frequencies, exchangeable sequences and degrees of belief are all *models* of the same measure.

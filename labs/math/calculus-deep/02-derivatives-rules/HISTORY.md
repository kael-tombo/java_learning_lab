# History: Derivatives and Rules

## Slopes before calculus (1630s-1637)
Fermat's adequality method of the 1630s found tangents and extrema
algebraically. Descartes published La Geometrie in 1637 with his own
circle method for normals. Both worked; neither defined a derivative.

## Newton and Leibniz, stated neutrally
Newton developed fluxions in manuscripts of the 1660s and 1670s and
published the Principia in 1687. Leibniz published his differential
calculus in 1684, with the integral paper following in 1686. Both
invented working calculi independently; the ensuing priority dispute
was adjudicated by a 1712 Royal Society committee report, a document
whose impartiality historians have questioned ever since. This file
takes no side beyond the publication record.

## The rules enter textbooks (1696-1755)
L'Hopital's Analyse of 1696 collected the power, product, and quotient
techniques into teachable form. Leonhard Euler's Institutiones calculi
differentialis (1755) systematized differentiation of exponentials,
logarithms, and trigonometric functions with the chain rule used
throughout.

## Rolle and the Mean Value Theorem (1691-1823)
Michel Rolle proved his theorem for polynomials in his 1691 Traite
d'algebre. Joseph-Louis Lagrange stated mean-value forms in his 1797
Theorie des fonctions; Cauchy proved the theorem in the form taught
today in his 1823 Resume. Differentiability implying continuity is
Cauchy's rigorization of what the 1696 textbook had assumed.

## What this lab inherits
Every rule drilled here, from the power rule to the chain rule, was
computing answers for a century (1684-1797) before Cauchy's 1823
proofs certified the Mean Value Theorem underneath them. Chain-rule
fluency with exponentials and trig, the lab's core drill, is Euler's
1755 curriculum still running unchanged.

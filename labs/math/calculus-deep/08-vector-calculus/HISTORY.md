# History: Vector Calculus

## Flux theorems first (1810s-1854)
Green's 1828 Essay on electricity stated the planar circulation-flux
theorem. Ostrogradsky presented the divergence theorem in the 1820s,
published in the early 1830s; Gauss used related flux arguments in the
1810s-30s. Kelvin described the circulation theorem in an 1850 letter
to Stokes, who set it as the 1854 Smith's Prize examination question,
fixing Stokes' name to it permanently.

## Quaternions to vectors (1843-1901)
Hamilton's 1843 quaternions contained gradient-like operators.
Maxwell's Treatise on Electricity and Magnetism (1873) used
quaternionic language for fields. Gibbs' 1880s notes and the 1901
Gibbs-Wilson textbook rewrote the content as divergence, curl, and
the theorems of the previous paragraph, the form this lab uses.

## Heaviside's engineering (1890s)
Oliver Heaviside independently developed vector operator methods in
his Electromagnetic Theory of the 1890s, including an operational
calculus engineers adopted decades before mathematicians justified
it. His notation for field operators overlaps heavily with Gibbs'.

## Forms unify everything (circa 1900-1930s)
Elie Cartan's differential forms, developed from around 1900 through
the 1920s, made gradient, curl, divergence, Green, Stokes, and Gauss
instances of one generalized Stokes theorem. De Rham's 1930s
cohomology theory explained when potentials exist globally, the deep
version of this lab's conservative-field checks.

## What this lab inherits
Div 12 and the two curl values at points, potentials verified on two
paths, Green confirmed at 2 pi both sides, flux at 4 pi both sides:
each equality is a 19th-century theorem; their unity is Cartan's.
Modern texts often present all three as one; the lab keeps them
separate because each arrived separately, Green first in 1828 and
Stokes last in 1854.

# History: Limits and Continuity

## Exhaustion (4th century BCE and Archimedes)
Eudoxus of Cnidus developed the method of exhaustion in the 4th
century BCE. Archimedes applied it with full force in works including
the Quadrature of the Parabola and the Method, trapping areas between
arbitrarily close upper and lower bounds: limits in everything but
name and notation.

## Tangents before limits (1630s-1670)
Pierre de Fermat's 1630s technique of adequality found maxima and
tangents without any limit concept. Descartes' La Geometrie (1637),
Cavalieri's indivisibles (1635), and Barrow's lectures (published
1670) pushed infinitesimal methods to the edge of rigor without
defining the limit itself.

## Calculus without foundations (1684-1820)
Newton's fluxions (manuscripts of the 1660s-70s, Principia 1687) and
Leibniz's papers of 1684 and 1686 computed limits fluently while
defining them loosely. L'Hopital's 1696 Analyse, written from Johann
Bernoulli's lectures, spread the techniques across Europe a full
century before anyone made them rigorous.

## Rigor arrives (1817-1860s)
Bernard Bolzano gave an analytic proof of the Intermediate Value
Theorem in 1817. Cauchy's Cours d'analyse (1821) and Resume (1823)
defined limits and continuity recognizably. Karl Weierstrass, in his
Berlin lectures of the 1850s and 1860s, cast it all in epsilon-delta
form, the version this lab teaches.

## What this lab inherits
One-sided limits, the squeeze theorem, discontinuity types, and the
Intermediate Value Theorem are the 1821-1860s settlement of questions
Archimedes posed and Fermat computed around. L'Hopital's rule keeps
its 1696 name with its 1820s hypotheses.

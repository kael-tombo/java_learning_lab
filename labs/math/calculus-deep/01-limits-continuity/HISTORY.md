# History: Limits and Continuity

## Ancient Origins

### Zeno's Paradoxes (c. 450 BCE)
Zeno of Elea formulated paradoxes that forced early thinkers to confront the
notion of infinite processes. In "Achilles and the Tortoise," Achilles gives
the tortoise a head start and must first reach where the tortoise was, but by
then the tortoise has moved forward. This infinite subdivision of distance
implicitly raises the question: does an infinite sum of ever-smaller steps
converge to a finite value? The paradox remained unresolved for two millennia
because the concept of a limit had not yet been formalized.

### Archimedes' Method of Exhaustion (c. 250 BCE)
Archimedes of Syracuse computed the area of a circle and the volume of a sphere
by inscribing and circumscribing polygons with increasing numbers of sides. As
the number of sides grows, the polygon's area approaches the circle's area.
This is an early implicit use of a limit, though Archimedes framed it as a
double reductio ad absurdum rather than a limiting process.

## 17th Century: The Birth of Calculus

### Newton and Leibniz (1660s–1680s)
Isaac Newton (1643–1727) developed his "method of fluxions," in which he
considered quantities as generated by continuous motion. He used the notion of
an "ultimate ratio" of evanescent increments — essentially a limit — to define
the derivative. Gottfried Wilhelm Leibniz (1646–1716) independently developed
calculus with his differential notation dx and dy, treating them as
infinitesimally small quantities. Both relied on intuitive notions of limits
without a rigorous definition.

### The Berkeley Critique (1734)
Bishop George Berkeley published "The Analyst" (1734), attacking the logical
foundations of calculus. He famously derided infinitesimals as "the ghosts of
departed quantities." This critique exposed the lack of rigor and motivated
later mathematicians to find a solid foundation.

## 19th Century: The Arithmetization of Analysis

### Cauchy's Cours d'Analyse (1821)
Augustin-Louis Cauchy (1789–1857) took the first major step toward rigor. In
his *Cours d'Analyse de l'École Royale Polytechnique* (1821), he defined the
limit of a variable quantity: "When the values successively attributed to a
variable approach indefinitely a fixed value, so as to end by differing from
it by as little as one wishes, this last is called the limit of all the
others." Cauchy also gave the first rigorous definition of continuity: a
function f is continuous at a if the difference f(a + α) − f(a) becomes
infinitesimal whenever α becomes infinitesimal.

### Bolzano's Contributions (1817)
Bernard Bolzano (1781–1848) published *Rein analytischer Beweis* (1817),
giving an early version of the intermediate value theorem and a definition of
limit that anticipated the modern epsilon-delta formulation. His work was
largely ignored during his lifetime.

### Weierstrass and the Epsilon-Delta Definition (1850s–1870s)
Karl Weierstrass (1815–1897) is credited with the fully rigorous epsilon-delta
definition of a limit, which he used in his lectures at the University of
Berlin. The definition: lim(x→a) f(x) = L means that for every ε > 0 there
exists δ > 0 such that 0 < |x − a| < δ implies |f(x) − L| < ε. This definition
eliminated all reference to infinitesimals and motion, grounding limits
entirely in the algebra of inequalities. Weierstrass also constructed a
function that is continuous everywhere but differentiable nowhere (1872),
shocking the mathematical community and demonstrating that continuity does not
imply smoothness.

## 20th Century and Beyond

### The Rise of Non-Standard Analysis (1961)
Abraham Robinson (1918–1974) developed non-standard analysis in 1961, providing
a rigorous foundation for infinitesimals using model theory and the concept of
hyperreal numbers. This vindicated Leibniz's intuitive approach, showing that
infinitesimals could be made logically sound.

### Computational Limits
The development of computers in the 20th century led to numerical methods for
estimating limits, though floating-point arithmetic introduced new challenges:
catastrophic cancellation, round-off error, and the difficulty of distinguishing
a slow divergence from a slow convergence.

- **Zeno of Elea** (c. 450 BCE): paradoxes of infinite division
- **Archimedes** (c. 250 BCE): method of exhaustion
- **Isaac Newton** (1643–1727): method of fluxions
- **Gottfried Wilhelm Leibniz** (1646–1716): differential notation
- **Augustin-Louis Cauchy** (1789–1857): first rigorous limit definition
- **Bernard Bolzano** (1781–1848): early epsilon-delta ideas
- **Karl Weierstrass** (1815–1897): modern epsilon-delta definition
- **Abraham Robinson** (1918–1974): non-standard analysis

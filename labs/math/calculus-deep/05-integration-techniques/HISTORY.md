# History: Integration Techniques

## Substitution and parts (late 1600s-early 1700s)
Substitution is the chain rule read backwards and appears in the
Leibniz correspondence of the 1680s onward. Integration by parts, the
product rule backwards, became standard in early-18th-century texts;
no single inventor is named because the papers of the priority
generation all use it.

## Partial fractions (circa 1702)
The Bernoulli-Leibniz correspondence around 1702 worked out the
decomposition of rational functions into integrable pieces. The
method assumed factorization of the denominator, a harder problem
that waited on 19th-century algebra.

## Trigonometric techniques (18th century)
Trig integrals and trig substitution grew through the 1700s as
textbook craft, systematized in Euler's Institutiones calculi
integralis (1768). The Weierstrass tangent-half-angle substitution
that finishes the family is 19th-century folklore attached to his name.

## Improper integrals (1823 and after)
Cauchy's 1823 treatment defined integrals over unbounded regions and
near singularities as limits. The p-test comparison for convergence
became standard in 19th-century analysis courses.

## Machines that integrate (1960s-1970)
Early symbolic integrators appeared in the 1960s as thesis-scale AI
projects. Robert Risch's decision procedure for elementary
antiderivatives, published around 1968-1970, drew the boundary of
what machines can do in principle; it underlies modern computer
algebra integration.

## What this lab inherits
Every antiderivative here is checked by differentiating back, the
same verification Leibniz used in letters: the technique proposes,
differentiation disposes.

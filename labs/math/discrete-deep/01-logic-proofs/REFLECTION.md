# Reflection: Logic and Proofs

## Question 1: Why Does Proof by Contradiction Work?

We prove P by assuming ¬P and deriving a contradiction. But why does this establish P? What logical principle justifies this? Consider: does proof by contradiction work in intuitionistic logic (which rejects the law of excluded middle)? What can you prove without it?

## Question 2: What Is the Difference Between ⊢ and ⊨?

The symbol ⊢ means "provable in a formal system" (syntactic), while ⊨ means "true in all models" (semantic). Gödel's completeness theorem says these coincide for first-order logic. But Gödel's incompleteness theorem says that for any consistent formal system containing arithmetic, there are statements φ such that ⊨ φ but ⊬ φ. Can you construct an example of such a statement? What does it mean for the foundations of mathematics?

## Question 3: How Do You Know When to Use Induction vs. Direct Proof?

Some statements have both a direct proof and an inductive proof. For example, "the sum of the first n odd numbers is n²" can be proven directly (Gauss's pairing argument) or by induction. What are the trade-offs? When is induction more natural? When is a direct proof more informative?

## Question 4: What Makes a Proof "Elegant"?

Mathematicians value elegance, but what does it mean? Is it brevity? Generality? Insight? Compare Euclid's proof of the infinitude of primes with Euler's proof using the divergence of the harmonic series. Which is more elegant? Why? Does elegance matter for correctness?

## Question 5: Can a Computer Find Proofs That Humans Cannot?

Automated theorem provers can find proofs that are too long for humans to verify by hand (e.g., the Robbins conjecture, proved by EQP in 1996). Does this change the nature of mathematical proof? If a proof is too long for a human to understand, is it still a proof?

# History: Logic and Proofs

## Ancient Foundations

**Aristotle (384–322 BC)** formalized syllogistic logic in his *Organon*, establishing the first systematic theory of deductive reasoning. His categorical syllogisms (e.g., "All men are mortal; Socrates is a man; therefore Socrates is mortal") dominated logical study for over two millennia.

**Chrysippus (279–206 BC)** developed Stoic logic, introducing propositional connectives (if-then, and, or, not) that form the basis of modern propositional logic.

## The Algebraic Turn

**George Boole (1815–1864)** published *The Laws of Thought* (1854), creating an algebra of logic where propositions take values 0 or 1 and connectives become arithmetic operations. Boole's work made logic amenable to symbolic manipulation.

**Gottlob Frege (1848–1925)** invented the predicate calculus in *Begriffsschrift* (1879), introducing quantifiers (∀, ∃) and variables. This was the first logic capable of expressing all of mathematics.

## The Foundational Crisis

**Bertrand Russell (1872–1970)** discovered Russell's paradox (1901), showing that naive set theory was inconsistent. This triggered the foundational crisis in mathematics.

**Bertrand Russell and Alfred North Whitehead** published *Principia Mathematica* (1910–1913), attempting to derive all mathematics from logic. The three-volume work took over 300 pages to prove 1 + 1 = 2.

**Kurt Gödel (1906–1978)** proved the incompleteness theorems (1931): any consistent formal system capable of expressing arithmetic contains true statements that cannot be proven within the system. This ended Hilbert's program of formalizing all mathematics.

**Alan Turing (1912–1954)** introduced the Turing machine (1936), formalizing the notion of computation and proving the undecidability of the halting problem.

## Modern Developments

**Jacques Herbrand (1908–1931)** and **Gerhard Gentzen (1909–1945)** developed proof theory, creating natural deduction and sequent calculus systems still used today.

**Automated theorem proving** emerged in the 1950s–60s with the work of **Martin Davis**, **Hilary Putnam**, and **Allen Robinson** (resolution principle, 1965). Modern SAT solvers can now verify hardware and software systems with millions of variables.

# History: Linear Transformations

## Determinants come first (1693-1750)
Gottfried Wilhelm Leibniz discussed elimination determinants in
letters of 1693. Gabriel Cramer published the general rule bearing
his name in 1750, in his Introduction a l'analyse des lignes courbes
algebriques. Determinants therefore predate matrices by a century:
for a long time they, not maps, were the theory.

## The word "matrix" (1850-1858)
James Joseph Sylvester introduced the word matrix in 1850 for an
array from which determinants could be formed. Arthur Cayley's 1858
Memoir on the Theory of Matrices turned the arrays themselves into
algebra: addition, multiplication, inverses, and the statement now
called the Cayley-Hamilton theorem for small cases.

## Rank and nullity (1868-1879)
Ferdinand Georg Frobenius developed the invariant theory of bilinear
forms and matrices through the 1870s, including the notion of rank as
an invariant. The rank-nullity relation grew out of this line: kernel
dimension plus image dimension equals domain dimension, for every
linear map between finite-dimensional spaces.

## Canonical forms (1870)
Camille Jordan's Traite des substitutions of 1870 gave the canonical
form for a linear operator over an algebraically closed field. Change
of basis by similarity, and the invariance of trace and determinant
under it that this lab checks numerically, belong to the same body
of 19th-century invariant theory.

## Maps as primary objects (1920s)
Emmy Noether's module theory in the 1920s made homomorphisms, kernels,
and images the organizing concepts across algebra. From then on most
textbooks present the matrix as the coordinate shadow of a linear map,
which is the viewpoint this lab takes throughout.

## What this lab inherits
The running 2-by-3 example (kernel spanned by one vector, rank 2)
exercises exactly the 19th-century toolkit: elimination for the kernel,
minors for independence, similarity for invariants.

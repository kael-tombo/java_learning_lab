# Dual Spaces and Tensors — Quiz (15 Questions with Worked Answers)

Duality, annihilators, the tensor product, contractions, exterior algebra, and
covariant vs contravariant indices. The theme: dual objects carry the *actions*,
tensors carry the *multi-linear algebra*.

---

## Q1 — The dual space
**Q.** Define $V^*$ and the evaluation map. Give a basis of $(\mathbb{R}^3)^*$.

**A.** $V^*=\text{Hom}(V,\mathbb{F})$, a vector space of dimension $\dim V$.
Evaluation: $\phi\in V^*$ acts as a function on $V$; the map
$ev:V^*\to\mathbb{F}^V$, $\phi\mapsto\phi$, embeds $V^*$ into all functions.
Basis for $(\mathbb{R}^3)^*$: the coordinate functionals
$e^1=(1,0,0),\ e^2=(0,1,0),\ e^3=(0,0,1)$ with $e^i(v)=v_i$.
Note $V\cong V^{**}$ (double dual), not $V^*$.

---

## Q2 — Double dual and canonical isomorphism
**Q.** Why is $V\cong V^{**}$, and what breaks for infinite-dimensional $V$?

**A.** The map $v\mapsto\hat v$, $\hat v(\phi)=\phi(v)$, is injective and
(using an algebraic basis) surjective, with inverse on the dual of a basis.
For infinite-dimensional $V$ the map is injective but **not** surjective: the
surjectivity is exactly the axiom of choice (in ZFC) / weak choice.
Analytically, reflexivity is the requirement that closed subspaces be
intersections of kernels of continuous functionals (Hahn–Banach).
Consequence: the phrase "the dual" is basis-independent only for finite
dimensions; for infinite dimensions you must say *algebraic* dual or
*continuous* dual, and for non-Banach norms you need reflexivity hypotheses.

## Q3 — Basis transformation of covectors
**Q.** Basis $b=\{u_1,u_2\}$, $b'=\{u_1+u_2,u_1-u_2\}$. Express the dual basis
$e'^1,e'^2$ in terms of $e^1,e^2$.

**A.** $e'^i(v)$ must satisfy $e'^i(u_j')=\delta^i_j$.
Write $e'^1=ae^1+be^2$: $e'^1(u_1+u_2)=a+b=1$, $e'^1(u_1-u_2)=a-b=0$ ⇒
$a=b=\frac12$.
$e'^2=ce^1+de^2$: $c+d=0$, $c-d=1$ ⇒ $c=\frac12$, $d=-\frac12$.
So $e'^1=\frac12(e^1+e^2)$, $e'^2=\frac12(e^1-e^2)$.
Transformations are dual with **inverse** matrices: vectors transform by
$P$, covectors by $(P^{-1})^T$. That's the whole covariant/contravariant story.

## Q4 — Annihilators
**Q.** What is $(S^\circ)'$ where $S^\circ=\{f\in V^*: f|_S=0\}$?

**A.** $S^{\circ\perp}=(S^\circ)' = S^{\perp\perp}=S$ (double annihilator
theorem). The proof uses the Hahn–Banach extension theorem: given $v\notin S$,
there exists $f$ vanishing on $S$ with $f(v)=1$, so $v$ is not in $S^{\circ\perp}$.
This is exactly the functional-analytic statement "closed subspaces are
intersections of kernels of continuous functionals", and it's how one proves
Riesz representation in Hilbert spaces.

---

## Q5 — Finite-dimensional dual is easy, infinite is not
**Q.** $V=\ell^2$; compare $\dim V$ and $\dim V^*$.

**A.** For a Hilbert space, $V\cong V^*$ **canonically** via Riesz:
$v\mapsto\langle\cdot,v\rangle$ — no basis needed, because the inner product
supplies it. For a Banach space, $V^*$ can be much larger (e.g.
$\dim(\ell^1)^*=\dim(\ell^\infty)$ — uncountable dimensions).
The practical difference: in a Hilbert space every continuous functional is
represented by a vector, so "prove it's bounded" becomes "check the vector is in
the space". Outside Hilbert spaces you lose this, and estimates must be done on
functionals directly.

## Q6 — Tensor product: bilinear forms
**Q.** Give a basis for $V\otimes W$ when $V,W$ have dims $m,n$.

**A.** $\{u_i\otimes w_j\}$, $mn$ elements, and $\dim V\otimes W=mn$. This gives
the key correspondence: bilinear forms $V\times W\to\mathbb{F}$ ↔ linear maps
$V\otimes W\to\mathbb{F}$ ↔ elements of $(V\otimes W)^*$. The construction:
$V\otimes W$ is generated by symbols $u\otimes w$ with bilinearity, and the
canonical map $V\times W\to V\otimes W$ is *surjective onto the symbols* but not
injective on pairs (which is why $\dim V\otimes W=mn\ne mn$ pairs mapping —
$\dim(V\times W)=mn$ too, but the map collapses: $(u_1,w_1)+(u_1,w_1)=(u_1,w_1)$, e.g.).
So the dimension coincidence is a coincidence of counts, and the map's kernel is
huge — that's the essence of the quotient construction.

## Q7 — Kronecker product
**Q.** Distinguish $A\otimes B$ (matrices) from $A\otimes b$ (vector-valued).

**A.** For $A\in\mathbb{R}^{m\times n}$, $B\in\mathbb{R}^{p\times q}$:
$(A\otimes B)\in\mathbb{R}^{mp\times nq}$ with entries
$(A\otimes B)_{(i,p'),(j,q')}=A_{ij}B_{p'q'}$. Spectral relation:
$\sigma(A\otimes B)=\{\sigma_i(A)\sigma_j(B)\}$ and
$(A\otimes B)^k=A^k\otimes B^k$. Vector version: $A\otimes b$
$(mp\times1)=(Ab)\otimes e_p$ — i.e. each column of $A$ scaled by $b$.
Applications: quantum channels ($A\otimes B$ acting on a bipartite state),
Kronecker-structured Hessian for tensor-product discretisations, and
tensor-product kernel matrices (separable kernels: $k(x,y)=k_1(x_1,y_1)k_2(x_2,y_2)$).

## Q8 — Contraction
**Q.** What is the contraction map, and is it nilpotent?

**A.** Contraction takes $V^{\otimes p}\otimes V^{*\otimes q}\to V^{\otimes(p-1)}\otimes
V^{*\otimes(q-1)}$: contract the first vector factor with the first covector
factor, $(\cdots)\otimes v\otimes\varphi\otimes(\cdots)\mapsto
\varphi(v)(\cdots)$. The map $V^*\to\mathbb{F}$ is itself the full contraction.
Structurally, contraction is what makes tensor calculus useful: it maps a
$(1,1)$-tensor (an operator) back to a $(0,0)$tensor (a scalar), which is the
trace when the indices match around a cycle. The whole Levi–Civita discussion
below is about when a contraction gives a nonzero number.

## Q9 — The metric and raising indices
**Q.** In $V\cong V^*$ given by the inner product, what happens to
$(1,1)$-tensors vs $(0,2)$-tensors?

**A.** The metric $g$ is a $(0,2)$-tensor: $\langle u,v\rangle=g(u,v)$. Contraction
with $g$ defines *raising* and *lowering* indices: $T^a{}_b=T^{ac}g_{cb}$.
Under a change of basis $g$ transforms covariantly ($g'=P^TgP$) while an
operator transforms as $A'=P^{-1}AP$ — so the **same** tensor has both a
$(0,2)$ and a $(1,1)$ representation, and only with a metric can you write an
operator as a bilinear form. Euclidean QFT: the metric lets you raise/lower
indices, isotropic metrics make $T_{ab}=T^a{}_b$; general relativity is
diffeomorphism-invariant, so indices are raised/lowered with an *inverse*
metric, and the contraction with the *inverse* metric is the covariant
derivative — the reason GR needs nontrivial index gymnastics.

## Q10 — The antisymmetric part and Grassmann algebra
**Q.** When does an operator equal its own antisymmetric part?

**A.** $\varepsilon(A)=\frac12(A-A^T)$ (antisymmetric) and
$s(A)=\frac12(A+A^T)$ (symmetric); these are projections,
$A=s(A)+\varepsilon(A)$, and $A=\varepsilon(A)$ iff $s(A)=0$ iff $A$ is
antisymmetric. Taking the antisymmetric part three times gives $3\varepsilon(A)$
(since $\varepsilon^2=-\varepsilon$, and $A$ commutes with $\varepsilon$), so
taking it **six times** returns $A$: $\varepsilon^6(A)=A$. This "antisymmetriser
raises order" behaviour is why Grassmann algebra works: the exterior algebra
implements exactly these operators. In conformal field theory this is the
star-triangle / cyclic anomaly story; in linear algebra it's just that the
skew-symmetric part is itself skew-symmetric.

## Q11 — Exterior algebra and determinants
**Q.** Relate $\bigwedge^2\mathbb{R}^3$ to vectors.

**A.** $\bigwedge^2\mathbb{R}^3\cong\mathbb{R}^3$ via the Hodge star
$(*)(v_1\wedge v_2)=v_1\times v_2$. In general $\dim\bigwedge^k\mathbb{R}^n=\binom nk$
($3$ for $\bigwedge^2\mathbb{R}^3$), and the top wedge
$\bigwedge^n\mathbb{R}^n$ is 1-dimensional. The map $\alpha\mapsto\text{Pf}(\alpha)$
($\bigwedge^2\to S^2V^*$) links the Pfaffian to $\det$: $\det A=(\text{Pf}\,\alpha(A))^2$
for symplectic $A$.

## Q12 — Exterior powers of an operator
**Q.** $\Lambda^k(T)$ for $T:\mathbb{R}^3\to\mathbb{R}^3$, $T=J_2(0)\oplus[1]$
(shear block and identity).

**A.** $T=I+N$ with $N^2=0$; $T=I+N+\Lambda^2N$, so $\Lambda^2T=I+\Lambda^2N$
(identity on $\bigwedge^2$). $\Lambda^kN=0$ for $k\ge3$ (degree-2 nilpotent).
But $\Lambda^k(I+N)\ne I+\Lambda^kN$ in general: it contains cross terms
$\Lambda^jN\wedge\Lambda^{k-j}I$, which survive for $k=3$. So
$(\Lambda^kT)(v_1\wedge\cdots\wedge v_k)=\sum_S(\prod_{i\in S}N v_i)\wedge\dots$.
This is why eigenvectors of $T$ don't just map to eigenvectors of $\Lambda^kT$ —
and it appears in the exterior power of a matrix (used in dynamics, and in the
Bryant theory of the Routh–Hurwitz problem).

## Q13 — $k$-forms and volume
**Q.** Why is a nonzero top-degree form a volume element?

**A.** $\bigwedge^n\mathbb{R}^n$ is 1-dimensional with basis
$dx^1\wedge\cdots\wedge dx^n$ (up to orientation). A nonzero $n$-form is
$\omega=f\,dx^1\wedge\cdots\wedge dx^n$; it induces a volume measure on
oriented charts. Under change of coordinates $\omega=\det(J)\,d\tilde y^1\wedge\cdots$
— i.e. the density transforms by the Jacobian, which is why integration of forms
is coordinate-independent (change of variables theorem).
One never *integrates* a $k$-form over an arbitrary $k$-set — you integrate it
over **chains** ($\int_{[0,1]}dx$, $\int_{C}\omega$ for curves, etc.), and
integrability (Stokes) is defined on chains. This chain/complex framing is the
foundation of de Rham cohomology and differential forms in physics.

---

## Q14 — Evaluating a specific 2-form
**Q.** Compute $\omega=xy\,dx\wedge dy$ integrated over the unit square $[0,1]^2$.

**A.** $\int_0^1\int_0^1xy\,dx\,dy=\frac12\cdot\frac12=\frac14$. In 3D,
$\omega=z\,dx\wedge dy$ integrates over the unit cube to $\frac12$. The
$1/n!$ factor is why the volume form appears: integrating the constant top form
$dx^1\wedge\cdots\wedge dx^n$ over a box gives $n!$ times the volume — the wedge
already carries the antisymmetry that permutations would duplicate.

## Q15 — Non-commutative quantum mechanics: why the tensor product changes
**Q.** What changes when $V\otimes W$ becomes a module over a non-commutative
algebra, and how is that reflected in matrices?

**A.** Scalars commute with everything, so a scalar multiple of a tensor is a
pure tensor. For non-commuting operators (quaternion action, or the tensor product
of operators $A\otimes B$ which satisfy $(A\otimes B)(C\otimes D)=AC\otimes BD$
with $AC$ *not* symmetric), scalars are no longer central: $(\alpha I)\otimes(v\otimes
w)\ne v\otimes(\alpha I)w$ unless $\alpha$ commutes with both. This is the
"non-commutative tensor product" and its failure is why $V_G\otimes W_H$ is not
in general a module over $G\times H$ for infinite groups.
The dual, representation-theoretic version: for the symmetric group, the
irreducibles are Specht modules, and Young symmetrizers/Young tableaux
correspond precisely to tensor products of $V_G$ with symmetrisation
operations. Partition algebra, Schur–Weyl duality, and the Plancherel asymptotic
all rest on this tensor-with-symmetry structure.
